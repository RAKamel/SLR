LLM-based cybersecurity systems range from advisory interfaces to agents that select actions, invoke tools, retain context, and revise their plans after observing results. General surveys describe reasoning, memory, action, and tool interaction as central components of this development~\cite{B1_Wang_2024,B2_Xi_2025,B3_Zhang_2025,B4_Qu_2025,B5_Chowa_2026,B6_Cheng_2026,B7_Xu_2025}. This review uses an operational boundary: a system must combine an LLM or foundation-model component with an agent loop, tool use, multi-step planning, memory, coordination, or action on its environment. A model used only for classification or text generation does not meet this boundary.

Existing reviews provide adjacent but incomplete views of the target evidence. Broad systematic reviews cover LLM applications across cybersecurity~\cite{S1_Xu_2025,S8_Hasanov_2024}; SOC-focused surveys emphasize automation and operational integration~\cite{S2_Habibzadeh_2026,S3_Srinivas_2025}; other syntheses examine agent security duality and privacy~\cite{S5_Xu_2026,S9_He_2025}, cyber-threat detection~\cite{S6_Chen_2024}, or transformer-based intrusion detection~\cite{S7_Kheddar_2025}. The present review instead centers the operational agent boundary and jointly codes task stage, offensive or defensive orientation, architecture, adaptation, human involvement, evaluation, and artifact scope.

Three distinctions are necessary for interpreting the evidence. First, architectural modularity does not automatically establish a multi-agent system: several tools or classifiers may be controlled by one agent. Second, retrieval and persistent memory alter the context available to a model without necessarily updating its parameters. CurriculumPT, for example, schedules tasks and retrieves accumulated experience without model fine-tuning~\cite{R0322_Wu_2025}. Third, generating a recommendation or corrected artifact differs from executing and validating a change in the protected environment. These distinctions affect both autonomy claims and the meaning of reported results.

The review separates operational task, architecture, and human oversight. Task stages cover detection, alert triage, threat hunting or investigation, cyber-threat intelligence, incident-response planning, remediation, reporting, and penetration testing. Offensive orientation describes the activity performed, including authorized penetration testing; it does not imply malicious intent. Human involvement is recorded at the points where people provide objectives, guide execution, approve actions, review outputs, or intervene. Autonomy and oversight remain descriptive fields because a categorical scale has not yet been finalized.
